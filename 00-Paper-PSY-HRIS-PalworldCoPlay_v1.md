That's a structural advantage the Sandbox framework can exploit.

**The paper's claim is not that Palworld is a better game than Minecraft or ARK for artifact forensics.** It is that Palworld is uniquely suited as the *bonded co-play entry substrate* for parent-child shared play, because the parent can enter the substrate as a contributing participant rather than as an incompetent liability. ## 2. Bonded Simulation as a Second Observation Modality

The Sandbox paper established that observation under F_EXT distorts the substrate being observed. A clinician watching a child in an evaluation room sees the defense layer, not the processing engine. This is the load-bearing structural reason for asynchronous artifact forensics.

Bonded Simulation is architecturally distinct from F_EXT observation, and this distinction is load-bearing.

Under F_EXT observation, the observer's presence *is* the environmental pressure the child's processing engine is responding to. The observer is outside the substrate, watching from a position of evaluative authority. The child cannot enter unmasked processing because the substrate they are operating in *includes* the observer as an active stress vector.

Under Bonded Simulation, the observer is *inside* the substrate as a fellow participant with their own routing axis, their own contribution role, and their own vulnerability to F_EXT if the situation destabilizes. The participants are not in an observer-subject relationship — they are in a shared-substrate relationship in which each is doing their own work through their own axis. The child's processing engine reads the parent's participation not as evaluation but as contribution.

This is architecturally the same distinction the SNSFL Structural Precognition foundation [9,9,1,0] makes between IMS (Identity Mass Suppression, applied to intrusive observation) and the sovereign anchor condition (co-presence at f = anchor). Under F_EXT observation the substrate is being suppressed; under Bonded Simulation the substrate is being shared.

**But Bonded Simulation only works if the parent is actually contributing rather than surveilling.** A parent who joins the child's Palworld session with the private agenda of "I am going to observe my child's routing axis" is running F_EXT dressed in participation clothing. The child's substrate will read the surveillance intent regardless of the surface framing. Bonded Simulation requires the parent to enter the substrate *to play*, not *to evaluate*. The routing axis reads itself through the play if the parent is actually present as a participant. It does not need to be extracted through observation.

This is why understanding the routing axis matters *before* the co-play session begins. The parent who knows their child is A-dominant can enter the session with a contribution role (background resource farming, keeping the base stocked, gathering the materials the child burns through) that lets the child pivot freely without breaking flow. That contribution role IS the bonded participation. The parent is not observing — they are contributing. And the child's routing axis expresses through how they use the resources the parent supplies. The recognition happens as a byproduct of playing well together, not as the goal.

## 3. Getting the Parent Into the Game

This section exists because if the parent bounces off Palworld in the first hour, none of the rest of the paper matters. Practical guidance is corpus-honest here: the parent is a person the framework is trying to help succeed, not an abstract audience.

**Platform selection.** Palworld is available on Steam, Xbox (including Game Pass), and PlayStation. The easiest co-play setup for parent-child play is **Xbox / PC Game Pass cross-platform** — the child on Xbox and the parent on PC (or vice versa) can join the same session via invite code with minimal technical setup. If both are on PC through Steam, the built-in Steam friends invite works fine. Dedicated server hosting is available for larger family groups but is not necessary for basic co-play.

**Input device.** Palworld supports both keyboard-and-mouse and controller. Parents who are not accustomed to PC gaming will find controller (Xbox or PlayStation) significantly more accessible than KB/M. The learning curve for controller in Palworld is minutes; the learning curve for KB/M can be hours. If in doubt, controller.

**First hour survival tips.** The opening area of Palworld is deliberately forgiving compared to ARK, but new players still commonly stall out on a few specific patterns:

- Catch the first Pal (usually a Lamball) as soon as the tutorial prompts. Do not skip. It provides the wool needed for early crafting.
- Build the crafting bench and the Pal-catching sphere workbench before wandering far from the starter area. Everything else depends on these.
- Assign captured Pals to the base by picking them up and dropping them inside the base radius. They will begin working automatically on tasks that match their suitabilities.
- Do not engage Alpha Pals (large boss-tier wild creatures marked with a special icon) until you have leveled up significantly. They will destroy an underprepared player quickly. Route around them.
- Eat food and sleep in a bed regularly. Sanity and hunger are not decorative meters.

**What NOT to do in the first session with your kid.** Do not die repeatedly to easy enemies. Do not accidentally destroy their base structures while trying to figure out controls. Do not "help" by placing walls, chests, or Pals into their base without checking first. Do not rename their Pals. Do not release their Pals. Do not take their Pals into combat without asking. When in doubt, ask them.

**When to admit you are lost.** If you cannot figure something out, ask the kid. This is not weakness — it is the correct move structurally. The kid is the substrate expert in Palworld the same way you are the substrate expert in your own PCorpus domains. Asking the kid to teach you is one of the most powerful bonding moves available in this framework, and it directly builds the PCorpus interaction surface the Outpost Pattern (§5) depends on.

**A note on time investment.** Palworld does not need to be a large time commitment to work as bonded simulation substrate. Two 45-minute sessions per week is sufficient to build meaningful co-play PCorpus. The paper does not recommend marathon sessions; short, regular, bonded sessions produce better outcomes than infrequent long ones.

## 4. The Four Routing Axes in Palworld Specifically

The Sandbox paper [9,9,3,46] §5 established the P/N/B/A routing axis portraits using Minecraft and ARK exemplars. This section provides Palworld-specific artifact and behavior examples for the same four axes. The routing framework is identical; the substrate expressions are Palworld-specific.

### 4.1 Pattern (P) Dominant in Palworld

**Artifacts:** Perfectly grid-aligned base foundations with no off-snap tiles. Storage chests organized by Pal element type or item category. Breeding pens arranged symmetrically. Base perimeter walls placed on precise chunk boundaries. Pal work assignments matched exactly to Pal suitabilities with no cross-assignments. A greenhouse where every planter is the same distance from the water source.

**In-game behaviors:** Will spend 30+ minutes destroying and rebuilding a base foundation because a single tile was placed off-grid. Categorizes Pals in the Palbox by type, generation, or work suitability rather than acquisition order. Refuses to release Pals whose stats "ruin the symmetry" of a completed collection.

**Support:** Respect the grid. Do not build inside their base footprint without checking alignment. If contributing to their base, match their existing pattern language exactly. This is the axis where the Outpost Pattern (§5) provides the most relief — a P-dominant player NEEDS their base to be theirs.

### 4.2 Narrative (N) Dominant in Palworld

**Artifacts:** Every Pal has a custom name. Pals have detailed backstories, roleplay roles ("Sparky is the base defender, Foxie handles supply runs"), and pedigree tracking across multiple breeding generations. Base structures are named as if they were buildings in a village ("the Foxparks barracks," "the Northern Watch"). Tribe log or in-game notes contain detailed session history and Pal biographies.

**In-game behaviors:** Will not send certain Pals into combat because "she's expecting kittens next week" (referring to a breeding pair). Remembers exactly which Pal came from which capture location and which parents. Distress when a beloved named Pal dies is real and structural — a live database entry has been irreversibly modified. Time spent in Palbox management often exceeds time spent building or combat.

**Support:** LEARN THE CANON. Ask about the pedigree. Ask about the backstory. When you catch a Pal that might be useful, ask before adding it to a breeding line. Do not rename, release, or reassign their Pals. Do not treat their Pal database as inventory management — it is a live relational database with emotional stakes.

### 4.3 Behavior (B) Dominant in Palworld

**Artifacts:** Elaborate base defense grids with layered defensive Pals, traps, and firing lanes. Raid-boss preparation setups with staging areas, ammunition stockpiles, and consumable buffers organized by expected use. Test arenas built for specific combat scenarios ("this is where I test Pal aggro range"). Complex kinetic base machinery — Pal-powered mills, forges, and production lines optimized for throughput.

**In-game behaviors:** Will engage Alpha Pals and world bosses deliberately, often solo, to test combat mechanics. Attempts unusual combat combinations ("what happens if I hit this boss with a stun weapon at the moment its shield drops?"). Bases oriented around defense and raid preparation rather than aesthetics. Frequent re-testing of trap grids and combat setups after game updates.

**Support:** Be the second gun in raids. Be the ammunition supplier for boss fights. Be the tester the kid runs experiments on ("stand here and let me see if this trap fires correctly"). Do not try to over-strategize; let the kid iterate. Do not tell them not to fight the boss — supply them with better tools to fight it more effectively.

### 4.4 Adaptation (A) Dominant in Palworld

**Artifacts:** Multiple partially-built bases across the map, each abandoned when a superior design resolved in simulation. Half-finished breeding programs replaced by newer breeding programs the moment a better mutation target became visible. Constantly rotated Pal teams — the "best team" changes weekly as new Pals get caught. Rapid iteration on base layouts, with entire builds dismantled and rebuilt in a new location within days.

**In-game behaviors:** Bores of any given project quickly, not because of lack of engagement but because the mental simulation has already resolved the endpoint and building it out is just execution. Will pivot to a completely new base site the moment a superior terrain feature is discovered. Constantly catching and evaluating new Pals, rarely settling on a "final team." Distress when forced to finish a project the simulation has already moved past.

**Support:** BULK FARMING. Ore, wood, Paldium fragments, Ancient Tech Points, whatever the kid burns through when they pivot to a new prototype. Keep chests stocked without being asked. Let them pivot freely. Do NOT ask them to finish something before starting the next thing — the pivot is not procrastination, it is the internal simulation having already resolved the current build's endpoint. Their PCorpus works by iteration, not completion. The prototype trail is the artifact.

## 5. The Outpost Pattern

This is the load-bearing structural contribution of this paper.

When multiple family members share a Palworld world, the temptation is to build one shared base together. This creates immediate structural friction: a P-dominant player will feel their base integrity violated every time an A-dominant player pivots and dismantles half a wall to try something new. A B-dominant player will build combat infrastructure the P-dominant player considers ugly. An N-dominant player will name Pals the B-dominant player then loses in a raid. Shared single-base architecture guarantees F_EXT for at least one of the participants.

The Outpost Pattern resolves this structurally.

**Definition:** On a shared Palworld server or world, each family member establishes their own autonomous outpost — a personal base with its own Pals, its own build aesthetic, its own resource focus, and its own routing axis expression. Outposts contribute to shared server resources through their owner's natural routing axis. Members visit each other's outposts as guests, not as co-owners.

**Structural properties:**

1. **F_EXT elimination.** Each participant has full sovereignty over their own outpost. The P-dominant player's grid stays untouched. The A-dominant player's constant pivoting affects only their own outpost. The B-dominant player's raid infrastructure does not clash with the N-dominant player's roleplay village. No participant is compressing their substrate to accommodate another's.

2. **Contribution differentiation.** Each outpost naturally specializes according to its owner's routing axis. The P-dominant outpost becomes the meticulously-organized breeding facility. The B-dominant outpost becomes the raid-preparation staging area. The N-dominant outpost becomes the storytelling hub with named Pals and detailed roleplay. The A-dominant outpost becomes the experimental prototype ground where new mechanics get tested. All four contribute meaningfully to shared server resources through their differentiated specializations.

3. **PCorpus interaction surface.** When a family member visits another's outpost, they encounter a substrate expression different from their own. This creates natural teaching moments — "how did you get your Pals to sort themselves that efficiently?" from an A-dominant visitor to a P-dominant host, or "why does your defense grid trigger from three angles?" from a P-dominant visitor to a B-dominant host. The teaching is peer-to-peer, not top-down. **This is where the kid can teach the parent something** — "hey if you use this ramp system your Pals will pathfind better" — without the interaction being framed as instruction. It is one substrate expert visiting another substrate expert's PCorpus.

4. **Bond without compression.** The family plays together in the same world, contributes to shared goals, and interacts across their outposts, without any participant having to compress their routing axis to accommodate another's. Bonding builds through the shared substrate, not through forced togetherness. This is what Bonded Simulation looks like architecturally: shared substrate, differentiated contribution, mutual PCorpus recognition.

**Implementation:**

- Establish the main/shared base as a neutral hub with communal chests, guild-shared crafting stations, and a shared Pal breeding facility if desired. This is nobody's territory — it is the shared surface.
- Each family member picks a nearby location for their personal outpost. Palworld allows multiple base sites per player (three by default), which supports this pattern directly.
- Outposts should be far enough from the main base and from each other that build styles do not visually conflict, but close enough that visiting is easy. A 500-meter radius from the main base works well.
- Each outpost's owner has full decision authority over their own build. Visitors are guests. Ask before placing anything, catching Pals from another person's territory, or using their crafting stations.
- Shared resource contributions happen through the main hub. If your outpost produces a surplus of ore, deposit it in the shared chest. If you need something the shared chest does not have, gather it yourself or ask the family member whose outpost specializes in producing it.

**This is not just a play strategy. It is the structural architecture that allows Bonded Simulation to work across mixed routing axes.** Without something like the Outpost Pattern, mixed-axis families run into structural F_EXT on every play session. With it, each participant's routing axis is respected and their contribution is naturally valued.

## 6. The Parent's Own Routing Axis

The parent has PNBA too. Understanding the parent's own routing axis is important because it determines what kind of contribution role feels natural to the parent, where friction is likely to arise, and how to structure the Outpost Pattern so both parent and child can flourish.

**Parent P-dominant + Child P-dominant:** Natural fit. Both value alignment. Watch for competition over "who has the more perfect base." Solution: outposts with different aesthetic themes.

**Parent P-dominant + Child A-dominant:** High-friction combination if not managed. The parent's need for permanence collides with the child's need to iterate. Solution: the Outpost Pattern is essential here. The parent's outpost stays orderly; the child's outpost is the pivot ground; the shared base is a neutral zone.

**Parent P-dominant + Child B-dominant:** Manageable. The parent's structural rigor complements the child's experimental combat testing. Parent contribution: build the fortifications the child stress-tests. Watch for the parent flinching at the child's willingness to destroy things.

**Parent P-dominant + Child N-dominant:** Excellent fit. The parent's rigor supports the child's need to track detailed canon. Parent contribution: help maintain the pedigree records, keep the story documentation organized.

**Parent N-dominant + Child N-dominant:** Deep bonding possible through shared canon. Both invest in the story. Watch for canon collisions when both parents contribute conflicting lore. Solution: agree on canonical ownership of specific narrative threads.

**Parent N-dominant + Child B-dominant:** Manageable with awareness. The N-dominant parent will mourn Pals the B-dominant child loses in raids. Solution: the parent maintains their own Pal roster with named favorites who never enter combat.

**Parent N-dominant + Child A-dominant:** Requires patience from the parent. The A-dominant child will not remember canon the way the parent wants them to. Solution: parent contribution is documenting the story the child is generating rather than expecting the child to document it themselves.

**Parent N-dominant + Child P-dominant:** Good fit. The parent's story-building complements the child's structural rigor. The N-dominant parent can name and canonize the buildings the P-dominant child perfects.

**Parent B-dominant + Child B-dominant:** Fun but potentially chaotic. Watch for escalating stress-tests that damage shared server resources. Solution: designate combat-testing outposts far from shared infrastructure.

**Parent B-dominant + Child anything-else:** The parent needs their own combat outlet or they will pressure the child toward combat play. Solution: parent maintains a dedicated combat outpost and does raid preparation independently, contributing raid loot to the shared base.

**Parent A-dominant + Child A-dominant:** Compatible but may burn through resources faster than either can gather. Solution: acknowledge that neither of you will finish anything; enjoy the shared prototype phase.

**Parent A-dominant + Child P-dominant:** The parent must be careful not to pivot changes to the child's base without checking. Solution: parent's outpost is the experimental ground; child's outpost is protected.

**The general principle:** the parent's contribution role should route through the parent's own axis wherever possible. A parent who tries to force themselves into a contribution role that clashes with their own routing axis will burn out. A P-dominant parent should not try to be the "bulk farming" contributor for an A-dominant child indefinitely — they need base-building or grid-organizing work that satisfies their own substrate. The Outpost Pattern provides the structural room for this: each participant contributes through their own axis while sharing the same substrate.

## 8. When Co-Play Breaks Down

Bonded Simulation can fail. Signs that the session is entering F_EXT territory:

- The child becomes visibly tense when the parent enters a specific area of their base or outpost
- The child begins directing the parent's actions defensively ("no wait don't do that, don't touch that, stay over there")
- The child logs off suddenly or claims to be tired shortly after the parent joins the session
- The child asks the parent to help with something specific but then becomes frustrated with the help
- The parent notices they are working harder to have fun than they should be

These are structural signals, not behavioral failures. They indicate that something in the session architecture has produced F_EXT for the child, the parent, or both. Common causes:

1. **The parent is surveilling rather than playing.** Even without saying anything, if the parent's internal orientation is "I am observing my child's routing axis" the child's substrate reads it. Solution: the parent enters the session to play. The routing axis reads itself if the parent is genuinely present as a participant.

2. **The parent is contributing through the wrong axis.** A parent trying to bulk-farm resources for a P-dominant child who does not need resources (they need base integrity respected) is contributing through the wrong axis. Solution: check the contribution matrix. Adapt.

3. **Shared-base architecture instead of Outpost Pattern.** If both are trying to co-own a single base, the mixed-axis friction will surface. Solution: implement the Outpost Pattern. Give each participant sovereign territory.

4. **The parent's own axis is being suppressed.** If the parent is spending three hours farming resources for the child while the parent's own substrate needs to be building or storytelling, the parent is running F_EXT. Solution: the parent's outpost is where the parent's axis expresses. The parent should spend time in their own outpost too, not only in service to the child's.

5. **Real-world F_EXT bleeding into the session.** A stressful day at school, an argument earlier in the day, a fight between siblings — these can carry into the co-play session and produce distress that reads as "the game is not fun" but is actually unresolved external stress. Solution: recognize that the game is not the source; do not push through; end the session gently and try again another day.

**Ending a session that has broken down.** Do not force the session to continue. Do not lecture about the routing axis or the framework. Just say something like "I'm going to log off, that was fun, thanks for playing with me" and log off. The next session starts fresh. The child's substrate does not need explanation; it needs the pressure removed. If sessions repeatedly break down, revisit §5 (Outpost Pattern implementation) and §6 (parent-child axis interaction) — the structural setup likely needs adjustment.

## 9. Conclusion

The Sandbox Baseline Diagnostic Framework [9,9,3,46] gave parents a way to read their child's routing axis through artifact forensics — the passive audit of persistent digital play environments. This paper adds a second modality: **Bonded Simulation through active co-play**, using Palworld's Pokémon-adjacent surface as the parent-accessible entry substrate.

The load-bearing structural contribution is the Outpost Pattern: an architecture in which each family member's routing axis is respected through sovereign personal outposts contributing to shared resources through differentiated specialization. This eliminates the mixed-axis F_EXT that shared single-base architectures produce, and creates natural PCorpus interaction surfaces where family members can teach each other from their respective substrate expertise.

The paper does not diagnose. It does not evaluate. It gives the parent working tools to enter their child's shared substrate as an actual contributing teammate rather than as a surveilling observer or an incompetent liability. Understanding the routing axis is not the goal — it is the vocabulary that makes contribution possible. The goal is bonded play that both participants find meaningful.

If your kid plays Palworld, you can enter their world. If you know their routing axis, you can enter usefully. If you know your own routing axis too, you can enter without losing yourself. And if you use the Outpost Pattern, you can share the substrate without either of you having to compress to accommodate the other.

That is what bonded simulation looks like when it works.

This paper is about how to play a video game together well.

- ❌ "Palworld is the best or only game for bonded simulation" — it is not. Palworld is used as the exemplar in this paper because of its specific structural properties (Pokémon-adjacent surface, accessible co-op, division-of-labor mechanics). Other games can support bonded simulation using the same PNBA framework. Substrate choice depends on the family's cultural context, age of the child, and platform access.

- ❌ "The routing axis is a personality type" — it is not. Routing axis is a substrate-architectural feature that determines what kind of HRIS processing the person does. It is not Myers-Briggs, it is not enneagram, and it should not be used to make predictions about the person outside of substrate-processing contexts.

- ❌ "The Outpost Pattern requires literal physical separation between family members" — it does not. The Outpost Pattern applies to in-game architecture within a shared Palworld world. It is a play-structure recommendation, not a family-life recommendation.

- ❌ "Understanding my kid's routing axis lets me predict all their behavior" — it does not. Routing axis is one substrate-architectural feature among many. Kids are whole people with contexts, moods, relationships, and free will. The framework provides vocabulary for one specific dimension of how they process, not a total behavioral model.

- ❌ "If I know my kid's routing axis I can fix their difficulties" — the framework does not promise fixes. It provides recognition scaffolds and contribution guidance. Real developmental challenges require real support systems, not just parent-side reframing.

- ❌ "This paper is a diagnostic tool" — it is not. It is a parent-facing co-play guide. If your child is showing characteristics that concern you, that is a reason to get them seen by a qualified professional. The framework can help you communicate what you observe once you are in a professional context. It does not replace the professional context.

**What the analysis does establish:**

- Bonded Simulation is architecturally distinct from F_EXT observation and produces different substrate expressions
- Palworld's Pokémon-adjacent cultural surface makes it uniquely parent-accessible as a bonded co-play substrate
- The Outpost Pattern eliminates mixed-axis F_EXT in shared-server family play by respecting each participant's routing axis through sovereign personal territory
- Parent-child routing axis interaction is predictable in general shape and manageable when both parties understand their own axes
- Understanding the child's routing axis lets the parent identify contribution roles that are actually useful rather than accidentally in the way

**Final anchor.** If you are a parent reading this because you want to be closer to your kid through their video games: welcome. The framework can help you enter their substrate as a genuine teammate. It cannot replace the ordinary work of showing up for them consistently, respecting them as a whole person, and asking them what they need. Nothing in this paper substitutes for those things. What the paper offers is vocabulary and structure that makes the showing-up more effective, and a play architecture that reduces the friction that mixed-axis families otherwise absorb without knowing why.

Show up. Play the game. Ask them to teach you. Build your outpost. Let them keep theirs.

The rest follows.
