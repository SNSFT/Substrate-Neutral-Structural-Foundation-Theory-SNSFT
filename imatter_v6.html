Each world is filled with tiny cubes called TL-cells. Each cube takes the density of the rock or metal at its spot, from the standard model seismologists use. Count the cubes, add up their weight, and the total has to match the real Earth.

Each TL-cell of edge h takes the Preliminary Reference Earth Model (PREM) density at its center. Mass, mean density, polar moment of inertia and the gravity profile are sums over cells, compared with the measured values.

Radius5,000 km
Stack layers from the center out. Each layer reaches to a share of the radius; the top layer always reaches the surface. The page works out the exact answer from formulas, then counts the cells, and the two have to agree.
Layers are constant density. Exact: M = Σ ρ·(4/3)π(r₁³ − r₀³), I = Σ ρ·(8/15)π(r₁⁵ − r₀⁵); a uniform ball gives I/MR² = 0.4 exactly. Material densities at surface conditions: iron 7.87, granite 2.70, basalt 3.00, water 1.00, ice 0.92 g/cm³; mantle rock 3.30 and compressed iron-nickel core 11.0 are typical planetary values.

SCALE

1 unit = 1 cm (Unreal Engine convention; glTF, Blender and Unity divide by 100 for meters). Geometry is stored as dimensionless TL-cell counts; one declared edge length per scene converts every count to physical units.

One double-precision frame keeps 1 cm out to about 9 × 10¹³ m. Larger scenes give each octree cell its own local origin, so 1 cm holds at any scale.

The moment of inertia tells how much weight sits near the middle. It is never put in by hand: it comes out of the count, so matching it on three different worlds is a real test.
Earth uses PREM directly. For the Moon and Mars, the core and crust come from published seismic and gravity models, the mantle density is solved from the measured mass, and I/MR² is then predicted by the count. Measured values are C/MR²; normalization conventions (polar C against mean moment, mean against equatorial radius) differ by up to about 0.4%.
